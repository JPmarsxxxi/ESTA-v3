# Move ESTA's vision judge onto Kaggle

The best zero-cost replacement for Claude's vision work in ESTA is **an open Qwen model from the 27B-35B tier (Qwen3.8-27B for quality or Qwen3.6-35B-A3B for speed), run as a batch job on Kaggle's free 2xT4**. A **small 4B Qwen on the 4 GB GPU** handles fast local triage, and **free hosted APIs (Gemini Flash-Lite first)** act only as an overflow lane that may change without notice. On published benchmarks this model tier matches or beats Claude Opus 4.5 at reasoning over images, reading on-screen text and understanding video. It also accepts short mp4 clips natively and is trained to recognise celebrities, which a "right person" check needs. Where it still falls behind Claude is the hard part of ESTA's task: holding to "mismatch when unsure" instead of agreeing with the prompt, weighing many weak clues together, and giving the same answer on every run. No public benchmark measures those things, so **before switching, build a labelled set of 150-300 judgements from your own sessions and measure the false-match rate against Claude.** Apple cannot help on this machine. The Vision framework, Foundation Models and MLX all need Apple hardware, and the only Apple weights that run on Windows (FastVLM, MobileCLIP2) are licensed for research only, which rules them out for monetised videos. Treat every quota and speed figure below as provisional. The researchers could not open most official pages, so many limits come from search snippets and third-party aggregators.

## Open 27B-class models match Claude on paper, but ESTA's task sits in the gap

On reported static benchmarks, the open models are no longer the weak link. Alibaba's Qwen3.5 card puts its flagship 397B model at **MMMU 85.0 and MMMU-Pro 79.0, against Claude Opus 4.5 at 80.7 and 70.6**, with GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Pro slightly ahead of both ([DeepLearning.AI](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/alibabas-latest-flagship-models-are-open-weights-moe-performers-in-sizes-from-less-than-1b-parameters)). The tier that fits ESTA's hardware is close behind. **Qwen3.6-27B reports MMMU 82.9, MMMU-Pro 75.8, VideoMMMU 84.4 and Video-MME 87.7** ([Qwen3.6-27B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B)). Gemma 4 31B reports MMMU-Pro 76.9 ([Gemma 4 model card](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/core/model_card_4?hl=de)). Qwen3.8-27B was released 2026-08-14 as a dense Apache-2.0 model with native image and video input, and it claims gains over 3.6-27B ([DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/alibaba-releases-qwen3-8-27b)), though its full vision table was not found. Reading text in the frame, which is exactly the scoreboard and caption check, is where open models lead. Kimi K2.5 reports **OCRBench 92.3 against Claude Opus 4.5's 86.5** ([NVIDIA NIM card](https://docs.api.nvidia.com/nim/reference/moonshotai-kimi-k2-5)), and even the 9B Qwen3.5 reports 89.2 ([Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B)). All of these numbers are vendor-reported. The same Claude model scores 70.6 on MMMU-Pro in Qwen's table and 74.0 in Moonshot's, so tables from different vendors cannot be compared line by line.

Human-preference data tells a less flattering story. On LMArena Vision in March 2026, **the best open model (Qwen3.5-27B, 1237) ranked #20, about 50 Elo below Gemini 3 Pro (1290)** ([codesota arena summary](https://www.codesota.com/arena/vision)). Open-ended questions from real users look much more like "does this clip show the shot I described?" than multiple-choice MMMU does. The research also found no benchmark for the behaviour ESTA's prompt depends on most: rejecting a frame that is on-topic but the wrong moment. The current prompt in `tools/assets/llm.py` asks the model to reject celebrations when the description says "under pressure", reject close-ups when it says "wide", and reject when the wrong player, scoreboard, venue or date shows. That is exactly where a 2026 paper finds VLMs "ignore visual detail in favor of semantic anchors" ([arXiv 2604.02486](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02486)). Model size matters a lot here. PhotoPrism's guide calls the Qwen3-VL 2B/4B variants "less predictable and consistent", says 8B "generally works well", and recommends temperature 0.01 with repeat penalty 1.2 to curb hallucination and looping ([PhotoPrism](https://d.photoprism.app/user-guide/ai/ollama-models)). Expect a 27B Qwen to match Claude at reading the frame and to trail it at saying no, and expect anything under 8B to trail it clearly.

For named people, the comparison runs the other way. Qwen3-VL's README says its pretraining aims to **"recognize everything — celebrities, anime, products, landmarks"** ([Qwen3-VL README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL/main/README.md)). A 2025 hands-on test found Qwen identified well-known figures while InternVL3.5-14B failed to recognise all but two of them ([minimaxir](https://minimaxir.com/2025/07/llms-identify-people/)). The research notes say Claude generally avoids identifying people by face, but no primary source for that policy could be fetched. For the "person X, not person Y" marker, a Qwen judge may therefore be more useful than Claude. How well it recognises lesser-known athletes is unknown, and that belongs in the evaluation set.

## A 4 GB card runs a 4B triage model, not the judge

The local GPU limits ESTA to the 2B-4B tier. **Qwen3-VL-4B at Q4_K_M uses about 2.8 GB of VRAM** with its vision projector ([codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen3-vl-4b-vs-qwen3-vl-8b-benchmarks-vram-guide/)), and llama.cpp's `llama-server` runs it natively on Windows. It provides an OpenAI-compatible endpoint that takes several `image_url` parts per message ([llama.cpp multimodal docs](https://www.mintlify.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/inference/multimodal)), so it slots in behind the existing four-frame calls. One newer option is MiniCPM-V 4.6 (1.3B), which claims Qwen3.5-2B-level results on hallucination and OCR benchmarks and has been in Ollama since June 2026 ([MiniCPM-V README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenBMB/MiniCPM-V/main/README.md)). Larger models technically load if their weights spill into system RAM, but too slowly to be practical. One user running a 35B-A3B model on a 4 GB laptop RTX 2050 got **about 1.2 tokens/s** ([lemmy](https://lemmy.4d2.org/comment/6479265)), and processing image tokens on CPU-resident layers is slower still.

No one has measured seconds per image on a real 4 GB card. Working from Qwen's 32x32-pixel-per-token encoding, a 1280x720 frame costs about 900 visual tokens, and downscaling to 512-640 px on the long edge brings that to 300-400. Shrinking frames is the biggest speed lever. Under that assumption, a resident 4B model should finish a 1-4 frame call with a short JSON answer in roughly 2-8 seconds. That beats spawning a `claude -p` session, but it is an estimate. Given the consistency problems above, the 4B model should do only one job: **drop obvious mismatches** (wrong sport, a screenshot, a black frame) **before they reach the real judge**. It should never be allowed to approve a clip on its own.

## Kaggle's 2xT4 is the free home for the 27B judge

Kaggle offers **two T4s (32 GB combined) or one P100, for about 30 GPU-hours a week**. Kaggle publishes no official figure, though, and sources disagree on whether sessions last 9 or 12 hours ([aimultiple](https://aimultiple.com/free-cloud-gpu), [gpuperhour](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits)). ESTA already drives Kaggle through `kernels push/status/output` for IndexTTS2 and ai-video, so a vision kernel follows a pattern the code already has. Colab is not an alternative: it bans remote proxies and web UIs on free runtimes ([Colab FAQ](https://research.google.com/colaboratory/faq.html)). Hugging Face's ZeroGPU gives free users only a few GPU-minutes a day ([HF ZeroGPU docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/spaces-zerogpu.md)).

The batch shape works like this. One dataset holds the downscaled candidate frames plus a JSONL of prompts. Offline vLLM runs over all of it with continuous batching, and the shared rubric of roughly 400 words goes first in each prompt so prefix caching processes it only once. The researchers' order-of-magnitude estimate for 500 calls (about 575k prefill tokens and 30k output tokens) is **5-15 minutes of GPU time for an 8B or 30B-A3B-class model and 3-5x longer for a dense 32B at 4-bit, plus 5-15 minutes for queueing, boot and model download**. No public notebook benchmarks this on 2xT4, so these figures are unconfirmed. At **roughly 0.5-1.5 GPU-hours per video**, the weekly quota covers many videos, but it is shared with voiceover and AI B-roll.

The real risk is the T4's age, not its memory. The T4 (compute capability 7.5) needs `--dtype float16`, has no FlashAttention 2, and in one vLLM issue a quantized Qwen3-VL checkpoint failed with **"Min capability: 80. Current capability: 75"**, with no fix posted ([vLLM #26297](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/26297)). The `kaggle-vllm` package ships a vLLM build validated for Kaggle with float16, eager mode and two-GPU tensor parallelism as defaults ([kaggle-vllm](https://pypi.org/project/kaggle-vllm/)). Choose an AWQ or GPTQ checkpoint, not FP8 or compressed-tensors. If vLLM still refuses, llama.cpp's CUDA build with a GGUF split across both GPUs is the fallback; it gives up some throughput but runs on sm_75. Prove this works with a one-hour spike before anything else is built, because whether Qwen3.6 or 3.8 vision runs under vLLM on a T4 is completely untested.

Native video input is the payoff for the third requirement. Qwen3-VL accepts a local mp4 or a list of frames with configurable `fps` (default 2) and timestamp-aligned position encoding ([Qwen3-VL README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL/main/README.md)). Qwen3.5 and later fold this into the main model ([Qwen3.5 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3.5/main/README.md)). Claude, by contrast, only ever sees the stills ESTA extracts. A 5-15 second candidate at 1-2 fps lets the judge check that the action actually happens, not just that one frame resembles it. Treat that capability with some caution: on the harder Video-MME-v2, Qwen3.5-9B-Think scored only 23.2 ([arXiv 2604.05015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05015)), so short-clip benchmarks overstate real video understanding. Style description from reference videos is the easier task, since shot type, pacing and colour grade need no instance-level precision. Open models should be closest to Claude there, and the 20-40 style calls can run in the same kernel.

## Free hosted APIs are an overflow lane, not a foundation

Several no-card free tiers could absorb a 600-call day, but every figure here comes from aggregators and needs checking in the provider's console. **Gemini 3.x Flash-Lite reportedly allows about 500 requests per day**, while Flash itself is stuck at about 20, a level a September 2026 Google forum thread confirms ([Google AI Developers Forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-8-flash-free-tier-20-rpd-is-too-limited-for-practical-evaluation/180609)). Groq reportedly serves Llama 4 Scout free at 1,000 requests per day with up to five images per request ([rapidevelopers](https://www.rapidevelopers.com/md/ai-api-limits-performance-matrix/llama-4-scout), [releases.sh](https://releases.sh/release/rel_9pZUmudpM5N-Hno7_IlTc-llama-4-scout-and-maverick-models-now-available)), but whether Groq still serves Scout in October 2026 is unconfirmed. Cerebras offers Gemma 4 31B with 1M tokens per day but only 5 requests per minute ([benchlm](https://benchlm.ai/md/free-tier/cerebras.md)). OpenRouter's free models are capped at 50 requests per day unless $10 has been spent ([klymentiev](https://klymentiev.com/blog/openrouter-free-tier)), which breaks the zero-cost rule.

Two facts argue against relying on these tiers. First, they get cut. **In December 2025 Google cut Gemini 2.5 Flash's free tier from about 250 to about 20 requests per day without notice** ([technobezz](https://www.technobezz.com/news/google-slashes-free-gemini-api-requests-to-20-per-day-2025-12-20-gl06)), and Chutes ended its free tier in February 2026 ([cheahjs #217](https://github.com/cheahjs/free-llm-api-resources/issues/217)). Second, Gemini's free tier may use prompts to improve Google's models, with human review ([bswen](https://docs.bswen.com/blog/2026-03-23-gemini-free-tier-data-privacy/)). That is acceptable for public stock frames but not for unreleased footage. Put an OpenAI-compatible client behind a fallback chain so that a quota cut means switching to the next provider, with no code changes.

## Apple's vision stack cannot do this job on a Windows PC

None of Apple's runtime technology runs on Windows. The Vision framework is a set of fixed-purpose computer-vision tools. Its aesthetics score runs from -1 to 1 and accounts for blur, exposure and composition ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/vision/calculateimageaestheticsscoresrequest.md)), and WWDC26 added a dense-text OCR tool. None of it can judge whether a frame matches a free-text description. Since WWDC26, Foundation Models accepts image attachments ([WWDC26 session 237](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/237)). However, Apple's own report places its roughly 3B on-device model level with small open VLMs, and its server model **behind Llama-4-Scout and GPT-4o** ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/apple-foundation-models-2025-updates)). The free Private Cloud Compute model also comes with an app entitlement, per-user daily quotas and eligibility only for apps under 2M downloads, and image input to it was not mentioned ([WWDC26 session 319](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/319/)). Apple's open weights (FastVLM, MobileCLIP2) do run under PyTorch on Windows. Their license, though, is "exclusively for Research Purposes" and excludes "any commercial exploitation" ([ml-fastvlm LICENSE_MODEL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apple/ml-fastvlm/main/LICENSE_MODEL)), which rules them out for monetised videos.

Apple hardware matters only as a box for running open models. A Mac with MLX can serve Qwen over the LAN, and community benchmarks report **Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B at about 41 tokens/s on a 32 GB M4** ([oMLX benchmarks](https://omlx.ai/benchmarks/fqyhj2ep)). If the user ever buys local hardware for this, a used RTX 3090 at $700-900 ([modelfit](https://modelfit.io/gpu/rtx-3090/)) runs the same 27B-35B tier faster and for less than a $2,000 64 GB Mac mini. Neither purchase is needed for the zero-cost plan.

## Recommended architecture and how to prove it before switching

The design keeps Claude's prompt and JSON contract and swaps only the backend in `tools/assets/llm.py`. Both `_call` and `_call_with_images` become calls to one OpenAI-compatible endpoint, chosen by a config value.

| Tier | Where | Model | Job | Expected latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Triage | Local 4 GB GPU, `llama-server` | Qwen3-VL-4B / Qwen3.5-4B Q4_K_M (or MiniCPM-V 4.6) | Reject obvious mismatches only; never approve | ~2-8 s/call (estimate) |
| Judge | Kaggle 2xT4 batch kernel, vLLM (llama.cpp fallback) | Qwen3.8-27B or Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, 4-bit AWQ/GPTQ | Final match/mismatch verdicts on 1-4 frames or a short mp4; style descriptions | ~20-40 min per video incl. boot (estimate) |
| Overflow | Hosted free tier | Gemini Flash-Lite, then Groq / Cerebras | Kaggle quota exhausted or kernel failure | Seconds; quotas unverified |
| Tie-break (optional) | `claude -p`, capped per video | Haiku | Only the judge's low-confidence cases | As today, on ~5% of calls |

This changes the asset flow from "judge each candidate as it streams in" to collect, triage, batch-judge, apply. The assets fetch gathers the top-k candidates per shot and extracts frames. Local triage removes the obvious misses, one Kaggle kernel judges everything that remains, and an apply step writes verdicts into `assets_progress.jsonl` so the final render pass picks them up. It is the same push/status/apply shape the ai-video skill already uses, and the early render's placeholders cover the wait. Use schema-constrained JSON decoding at temperature near zero, and have the model fill evidence fields first: OCR'd text, the person it recognises, venue cues, then `matches`. One existing bug must be fixed before switching. On any exception, `validate_frames` currently returns **`{"matches": True, "confidence": 50}`**, which approves a clip whenever the call fails. A new backend will fail in new ways, so failures should come back as an explicit `unvalidated` result, never as a match.

Keep Claude in the loop until a measured comparison says it can go. Build a set of 150-300 cases from existing sessions. Each case holds the frames or clip, the shot description, the instance markers, and a human verdict. Weight the set towards hard negatives: same teams but the wrong match, celebration where the shot calls for pressure, a contradicting scoreboard, a lookalike player. Add a slice of lesser-known athletes. Run current Claude once as the baseline, then each candidate model at its real quantization level, because no data exists on how 4-bit quantization changes VLM judgements. The deciding metric is **the false-match rate on hard negatives**, with recall, valid-JSON rate and repeat-run agreement next. Switch the judge when the open model's false-match rate is within a few points of Claude's. Until then, keep the tie-break tier generous.

Several figures above could not be checked against primary pages and should be confirmed first:

| Item | Status |
|---|---|
| Kaggle quota (~30 h/week) and session length (9 vs 12 h) | No official number; sources conflict |
| vLLM running Qwen3.6/3.8 vision on T4 (sm_75) | Untested; quantization schemes may need sm_80 |
| Kaggle batch time per video and 4 GB local seconds per call | Estimates from token arithmetic, not measurements |
| Gemini Flash-Lite 500 RPD, Groq Scout 1,000 RPD, Cerebras limits | Aggregator snippets; Groq model availability unconfirmed |
| Qwen3.6/3.8, Gemma 4 benchmark numbers | Vendor-reported, read from search extracts |
| Claude's face-identification behaviour | Not sourced; test it in the eval set |
| Kimi K2.5 and MiniCPM weight licenses | Not checked |

## Conclusion

The question of whether an open model is as good as Claude matters less than it seems, because ESTA was never using Claude for its strengths. Each judgement starts a full agent session to answer a narrow classification question with a fixed rubric and a JSON reply. That job suits a batch-run open model, and the open model brings two things Claude cannot here: real video input and willingness to name public figures. What Claude provides that no benchmark captures is reliable scepticism, and that is precisely what the labelled set measures. Built once, that set also becomes ESTA's regression test for every later prompt or model change.

Strategically, Kaggle-first makes ESTA's main free resource carry three workloads: voiceover, AI B-roll and now vision. The 30-hour weekly budget becomes the real constraint on how many videos come out each week. The hosted-API overflow lane and the optional Claude tie-break are worth keeping because their quotas are separate, not because they are better.
